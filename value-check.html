Perceptual lightness (CIE L*), not your phone's saturation slider — a truer greyscale, because it weights the channels the way an eye does. Posterise collapses it into bands: if two materials land in the same band, they're the same value.

The histogram — where your values actually sit

Left is black, right is white. The shaded band is 40–60% — the mud zone. Work that lives mostly in there reads flat in photographs and across a display table, however nice the colours are up close.

What this can and can't tell you

It measures this photograph. Exposure, white balance, your light source and your backdrop all move these numbers, so treat them as a reading of one photo rather than a verdict on the model — and compare like with like by shooting in consistent light. Findings marked ◉ are camera problems, not painting problems. A wide range is also not automatically good: a deliberately low-key piece is meant to be compressed dark. The number is a prompt to look, not a score.

Now the part no algorithm can do
  1. Can a stranger point at the light source? Look at the greyscale panel and nothing else. If the highlights don't agree on a direction, they're decoration rather than lighting.
  2. Can you tell the materials apart? Push posterise to 4 or 5. If skin, leather and cloth collapse into the same band, you have a value problem wearing a colour costume.
  3. Does the focal point hold? Squint at the greyscale. Whatever your eye lands on first is your focal point — whether or not you intended it to be.
